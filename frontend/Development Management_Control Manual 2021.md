## Foreword

This Handbook focuses on the Development Management (Control) and Building Plan Appraisal procedures in local authorities. It should be read in conjunction with the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12], Urban Councils Act [Chapter 29:15], Model Building By-Laws and other allied statutes, guidelines, and standards issued by national government from time to time.

This Handbook was prepared with the objective of providing an easy reference to development management practitioners in Zimbabwe's rural and urban local authorities. Ultimately, it is expected that this initiative will aid in promoting uniformity in development management practice and standardisation in the country. It is therefore an important resource material for the newly qualified development management/control practitioner and a good reference for an experienced one.

The document draws on the work done in developing a development management/control manual for the Rural Areas of Zimbabwe in 1990 (Government, 1990). The financing of the aforesaid manual was provided by the Swedish International Development Agency (SIDA) at the request of the then Ministry of Local Government, Rural and Urban Development through the Urban Development Corporation. The adaptation of the Manual for use in urban areas was financed by the United Nations Development Program through the "Strengthening Local Government Service Delivery Capacity for Sustainable and Inclusive Development" 2016–2020 program. Further work on the Handbook was supported by GIZ under the Strengthening Service Delivery Project (No. 2018.2254.3). In all instances the processes involved experienced sector professionals and competent facilitators.

The production of this Handbook was undertaken by a group of development management/control practitioners drawn from urban and rural local authorities, the Ministry responsible for local government, University of Zimbabwe's Department of Rural and Urban Planning, Zimbabwe Institute of Regional and Urban Planners, Urban Development Corporation and other organisations. These included Town Planning Practitioners, Public Health Practitioners, Housing and Community Services Practitioners, Municipal Engineers and the Academia.

The first UNDP-supported process was facilitated by Daniel Chirundu (Director of Health, Kadoma City) while the GIZ-supported work was facilitated by Kudzai Chatiza (Development Governance Institute). To all those who contributed resources and technical guidance directly or indirectly, I am indebted.

## Preface

Government of Zimbabwe's Vision 2030 of attaining an Upper Middle-Income Economy status requires comprehensive spatial planning and development in compliance with relevant state policies, constitutional provisions, sustainable development goals (SDGs and the New Urban Agenda), national planning law and the competent administration of development management procedures.

Compliance with these instruments requires that national and local government officials establish and utilise procedures that allow diligent development management. This enables avoidance of any breaches of procedures while assuring citizens and land developers that where mistakes occur they will be detected and corrective actions taken speedily.

The Ministry of Local Government and Public Works supports urban and rural local authorities to run development management/control matters in keeping with the law. It performs this function in close collaboration with other strategic Ministries of the Government of Zimbabwe. The Ministry's performance is guided by relevant national policies, the Constitution of Zimbabwe, and other laws. It is this institutional framework that guided the production of this Development Management/Control Handbook.

The purpose of the Manual is to enhance the integrity of development management/control frameworks of all local authorities in Zimbabwe. It outlines the roles of local and national officials in development management. It captures all the key functions critical for efficient and effective local spatial planning and management of the built environment across the whole land development value chain. The procedures for executing each key function are outlined in a manner that will enhance transparency, reduce conflict, and ensure predictability in development management/control.

The primary users of this Manual are national and local government officials, including policy makers (Councillors) tasked with performing policy development, implementation, and evaluation of development management/control functions. Their roles and relations are defined in a manner that will enable easier assessment of performance and improve decision making processes. The Manual will be particularly useful guidance for Councillors in undertaking their policy making and oversight functions.

Citizens and residents will also find the guidance provided in this Manual useful. By referring to the Manual they will find it easier to navigate the different stages involved in holding their local authorities to account on development management/control matters. This will help address challenges experienced in the past where public resources were lost, efforts misdirected, and some built environment actors felt victimised where demolitions and evictions were ordered.

## 1.0 Introduction

Zimbabwe has a history of developing and managing sustainable settlements that are safe, inclusive and resilient. Their development and management has involved considerable state investment in infrastructure development guided by clear policies and standards.

National macro-economic policy frameworks have clear focuses on infrastructure development and support towards utilities, underpinning the importance of well-planned and sustainable settlements in the country. The need to maintain this investment in infrastructure, and ensure effective and systematic management of development in a professional and inclusive manner, is widely recognised. Improvements to the physical environment — in particular promoting order, health, safety, amenity, convenience and general welfare in the public interest — have lagged behind in recent years.

There are a number of emerging issues that are impacting on development management/control practice in the country. The Constitution requires that there are checks and balances in processing evictions and demolitions through seeking a Court Order if the demolition is not effected voluntarily by the developer. New land uses such as Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) filling stations at business centres, mobile phone base stations, solar farms, urban agriculture, open air worshipping, and car washes among others have evolved with time. These require Local Planning Authorities to be proactive.

There is therefore a need for a paradigm shift. The environment now requires that we transition from "development control" to "development management" underpinned by meaningful community participation. This will help with meeting community aspirations within human settlements. In this regard, competent policy makers and spatial planning practitioners are needed. All Local Authorities should therefore ensure that adequate and competent town planning professionals are available as they are important actors in development management.

### 1.1 Institutional Framework

The Manual upholds people's basic rights that are enshrined in the Constitution of Zimbabwe, particularly the national objectives in Chapters 2 and 4. In addition to the Constitution of Zimbabwe, the following legislation is important to the everyday functioning of a Local Authority with respect to spatial planning and development management/control:

- The Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12]
- Urban Councils Act [Chapter 29:15]
- Public Health Act [Chapter 15:09]
- The Factories and Works Act [Chapter 14:08]
- Liquor Licensing Act [Chapter 14:12]; and
- The Model Building By-Laws (1977)
- Local authority by-laws and instruments that impact on development management.

In addition to using relevant statutory instruments, preparation of this Handbook drew from other publications, in particular Department of Physical Planning Guidelines, the Ministry of Health and Child Care Minimum Requirements for Premises, by-laws and best practices from various local authorities. This Guideline should therefore assist in simplifying existing development management/control instruments by providing a simple job aid to be used by urban local authority development management/control practitioners.

Current dynamics in society require that development management/control is looked at through new lenses. It is important that the impact of development management/control actions aim to create "attractive, comfortable and convenient environments". Using a systems approach, it then follows that development management/control outcomes should include aesthetic, convenient, socially inclusive and safe settlements in which there is compatibility with existing developments. The output of any development management/control process should therefore arise from public consultations as a basis for examining viable options to ensure compliance (both voluntary and mandatory compliance) with existing plans and enforcement. To realise impacts at national level there is need for adequate inputs at local level. Development management inputs include:

i. Formulation of policies.
ii. Having appropriate legislation.
iii. Formulation of standards and benchmarks.
iv. Ensuring that competent human capital is in place, effectively organised and equipped to perform.
v. Designing and executing relevant human capital development (capacity building) initiatives.
vi. Ensuring that Local Planning Authorities embrace appropriate Information Communication Technology in development management/control.

### 1.2 Defining Development and its Management (Control)

The above institutional frameworks frame the definition of Development and its Management/Control. Development in this Handbook relates to the alteration of the physical environment taking place within the context of a system of planning and management. According to Section 22(1) of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12], development is "the carrying out of building, engineering, mining or other operations in, on, over or under land, or making of any material change in the use of buildings or other land".

Development management/control is the process by which a Local Planning Authority regulates and manages land use, construction and use of buildings. Therefore, all areas under the jurisdiction of a Local Planning Authority should have a development plan showing the existing and future use of the land in different land use categories. Examples of the broad land use categories include:

- Commercial (shops and offices)
- Industrial
- Residential (housing)
- Recreational
- Institutional (public buildings, community halls, etc.)

The land uses should be arranged in a systematic manner so that conflicts do not arise. This includes assigning separate zones for major uses as well as consciously mixing those uses that can complement each other. The mixing of developments also involves efforts at building social, economic and other forms of resilience within a settlement. Conflict may arise, for example, where a grinding mill or tannery is located next to or within a residential area without the necessary safeguards. When a new development takes place, it should follow the development plan as closely as possible. This development should be managed by the Local Planning Authority. The Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12] Part III Section 10(1) designates Local Planning Authorities in Zimbabwe.

#### 1.2.1 Objectives of Development Management

The main aims of development management/control are:

i. To ensure that development takes place within the designated land use, e.g. residential, commercial etc.
ii. To protect people and property by ensuring that buildings are safely constructed, that living conditions are healthy and that the environment is free of pollution.
iii. To protect the environment by setting conditions in development permits.
iv. To improve the standard of development by improving the landscape of planned areas and the quality of buildings.
v. To promote development through the coordination of private and public investment for the more efficient use of land.

Thus, development management/control is not only a control mechanism but a promotional and value-adding tool that ensures development is carried out in a manner that will not create a danger to the public, adversely affect the health of the community, cause a nuisance, or damage the environment.

#### 1.2.2 Mechanisms for Development Management

A Local Planning Authority has the responsibility to ensure that its area of jurisdiction is developed properly and managed economically so that the rights of denizens are protected and public resources are well-spent. Any natural or juristic person and institution intending to carry out development must first get the permission of the appropriate Local Planning Authority. This permission, sometimes called the planning permission, is issued in the form of a Development Permit by the Local Planning Authority.

Undertaking development without a permit is an offence. The Local Planning Authority is empowered to take legal action against offenders. Developers need to know when a development permit is required. The Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12], together with the General Development Order and Special Development Order, define the development permit requirements.

#### 1.2.3 Circumstances where Development Permits are needed

A development permit is required when:

a) Any building or mining operations are carried out on, over or under the land, except where permitted by the Mines and Minerals Act [Chapter 21:05].
b) The deposit of waste materials on any land.
c) Changes to the character, use and intensity of use of the land or buildings.
d) The construction or change of any enclosure.
e) The construction of an out-building which is greater than 30 m² or 10% of the floor space of the principal building, whichever is the less.
f) The use of any vehicle or similar object (e.g. caravan) as a building for longer than six months.
g) Any addition/extension to a dwelling.
h) Carrying out alterations which affect the external appearance of a building.
i) Changing a single family dwelling to enable it to be used as two or more separate dwelling units/flats.
j) Outbuildings used for human habitation or those not incidental to the use of the dwelling house.
k) Building a swimming pool.
l) Any development which obstructs or limits visibility at or near bends and road junctions or involves a new access to a road.
m) The display of an advertisement on any land or external part of buildings, except:
   - i. Advertisements not visible outside enclosed land.
   - ii. Traffic signs and statutory advertisements.
   - iii. Functional advertisements of a local authority, statutory body or public transport undertaking.
   - iv. Advertisements for identification, direction or warning.
n) Any development which conflicts with conditions of a title deed or permit, an approved plan or an order.
o) Any development within environmentally sensitive areas, e.g. wetlands and water courses.

#### 1.2.4 Circumstances where a Development Permit is not required

According to Section 22 of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12], a permit is **not** required in respect of:

I. Activities not defined as development in Section 22 of the Act.
II. Existing development which enjoys existing use rights.
III. Activities referred to in a development order.
IV. The temporary construction of a building or use of land required in connection with operations which have been granted a development permit.
V. The development by a local authority of the provisions of any approved plan/scheme or ministerial directive/approval.
VI. Buildings providing a public service but not for human habitation, e.g. bus shelter.
VII. The provision and maintenance of public utilities/services, military buildings, silos etc.
VIII. When the existing and proposed development are in the same use group, e.g. a hardware business being replaced by a clothing shop, converting a dry cleaner's reception depot into a shoe repair shop, etc.
IX. The maintenance of any building.

## 2.0 Development Permissions

In terms of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12], a permit is required before any development or a change of use of any property is undertaken. Section 26 of the Act lays down the permit application procedure. The application is made to the Local Planning Authority. It is a very detailed application. A person about to embark on development must obtain a stand or plot and/or a lease agreement from the local authority.

In urban areas, stands are surveyed and pegged for individual use, but they must be subject to a lease agreement between the developer and the local authority. The lease agreement does not give the developer any rights to begin building; however, it must be obtained before an application for development is made. An application for development is made to the Local Authority by a developer with the following:

### 2.1 The Application Form (TPD)

The forms must be completed in full by the person who wishes to carry out the development and must be signed by the owner of the land or lessee. If the property has a real right registered against the title deed, the title deed of the property concerned should be submitted and the written consent of bond holders will be necessary. Forms TPD must be kept in the Council's offices so that they are available free of charge to members of the public. A sample TPD form is shown in [Annexure 1](#annexure-1--sample-tpd-form).

### 2.2 The Application Fee

The application fees are determined by Council by resolution annually.

#### 2.2.1 Building Plans

Building plans must be drawn to scale showing all the details of the proposed development. The Local Authority usually requires 3 or 4 copies of the building plans. A Local Authority can be approached by a developer in 3 ways:

i. The developer can write to ask if the development proposed requires permission. The Local Authority would have to advise the developer accordingly.
ii. Before spending money on the preparation of building plans, a developer may wish to find out if the development proposed would be issued with a permit. A preliminary planning permission application is made which will include the application fee and as much information on the proposed development to indicate the type, size and approximate cost.
iii. The developer will submit a TPD 1 form with the application fee and building plans as required.

A Local Authority must open and maintain a development register for all development applications. A sample development register is shown in [Annexure 2](#annexure-2--specimen-development-register). All details of the application and each step in the processing of the application must be entered in the Register. The development register should be available for inspection by members of the public during normal working hours, free of charge.

### 2.3 Processing a Development Application

Development applications are usually determined by the Works and Planning Committee of Council. Town planning officials prepare each application for the Committee's determination. The officials scrutinise applications in detail to provide sound advice to the Committee. It is important for the correct procedures to be followed in processing applications. The following procedures are suggested:

**Step 1:** Note the date of receipt of application, applicant's name and proposed development in a Development Register.

**Step 2:** Examine application contents to decide completeness of application. A complete application includes, among others, the following:
   - i) A full description of technical aspects of the proposed development with appropriate illustrations or drawings as needed.
   - ii) Justification either in terms of relevant policy and demand for the development being proposed.
   - iii) Proof of funding.
   - iv) Clarity on land rights, and
   - v) Other policy considerations that a Local Planning Authority may stipulate from time to time.

**Step 3:** If the application is complete, note the amount paid (the application fee) and forward a receipt and an acknowledgement to the applicant within 5 working days. Advise the applicant that if they do not hear from the Local Planning Authority within 4 months then the application will have been deemed approved. An example of a standard letter used in the City of Bulawayo is shown in [Annexure 3](#annexure-3--typical-acknowledgement-letter-city-of-bulawayo).

   If the application is incomplete, send all the contents back to the applicant, advising them of the application's incompleteness and how they can remedy or improve their application for proper processing by the Local Planning Authority.

   The notification of application completeness shall be accompanied by standard procedures for processing of development applications. The notification/letter shall refer to relevant procedures depending on the nature of the application. Some of the information may include procedures for public consultations with owners of abutting properties as well as other interested or affected persons who will be notified as required. [Annexures 4](#annexure-4--notification-of-abutting-properties) and [5](#annexure-5--advertisement-for-publication) show relevant examples on how consultations are facilitated.

**Step 4:** In the event of notices being required, these shall be published by the applicant at their expense in one issue of a newspaper circulating in the area. A certificate by the newspaper in which this notice appears shall be furnished by the applicant to the Local Planning Authority within seven days of the advertisement, otherwise consideration of the application may be delayed.

   For complete applications this step involves production and submission of a report by the Planning Unit for consideration by the relevant Council Committee. The Report shall cover spatial and economic planning matters only.

**Step 5:** The Works and Planning (or such other relevant) Committee of Council meets to decide on the application. An appropriate officer shall present their assessment of the application making technical recommendations as to the suitability of the proposed development. The Committee adjudicates on the matter and either recommends adoption or rejection of the Planning Unit's recommendation regarding planning permission.

**Step 6:** This stage involves presentation to Council of the application by the Committee with its recommendations. Upon adoption of the Committee's recommendation by Council, the Office of the Town Clerk shall initiate appropriate implementation of the Resolution.

**Step 7:** This involves processing and communicating the decision of Council with an applicant. Where the application is approved, the Office of the Town Clerk, in close liaison with the Planning Unit/Office, shall process issuance of a Development Permit with relevant planning conditions attached.

   Where not granted, the communication shall be made with the applicant within seven days of the Resolution being made, with reasons for refusal of the Development Permit. The communication shall also state the applicant's right to appeal the decision at the Administrative Court within 30 days of the decision.

**Step 8:** This step involves implementation of the development in compliance with the Permit issued. The Local Planning Authority shall take all necessary measures within the limits of resources available to it to assist the applicant to follow all proper spatial planning and development procedures.

### 2.4 Determining an Application

Considering the merits of a planning application is the most important aspect of processing a planning application. The key factors that are looked at — based on the application or any additional inquiries with the applicant or other appropriate sources of information — are outlined below.

#### 2.4.1 Building Plans

These must be properly drawn. The building proposed must look reasonable in terms of its construction, surrounding buildings and environment. The plans should be made available to all relevant departments within and outside Council, applying ease of doing business, which may require holding "One-Stop-Shop" sessions where all those required for purposes of appraising applications meet together. Such a one-stop shop will comprise of:

i. Town Planning
ii. Land Survey
iii. Traffic and Transportation
iv. Highways and Works
v. Health
vi. Fire Engineering
vii. Housing, and
viii. Other public institutions such as water, power and telecommunications utilities, education, the Factories Inspectorate and others as relevant to the applications being appraised.

#### 2.4.2 Conflict of Use

The proposed development should not interfere with established land uses. Wherever possible it is important to segregate different land uses — to maintain shops with shops and houses with houses, etc. — where no appropriate measures are instituted as part of a Development Permit. This is because it would be bad planning practice and a nuisance to allow a liquor outlet to be developed beside a place of worship, e.g. a church, synagogue or mosque.

#### 2.4.3 Multiple Stand Use

Multiple stand use is allowed where the uses fall in the same use group and are appropriate in terms of the land use zone in which the stand is.

#### 2.4.4 Conformity with an Approved Layout

The proposed development should fit within a layout prepared and approved for the particular area. A layout provides planned distribution of land uses (housing, industry, commercial, etc.) that complement each other and are designed with regard to the future cost-effective provision of services within a framework of environmental fit and sensitivity. Approved layouts should be respected in every regard with particular consideration of stand sizes, land uses and planning principles established for the area.

#### 2.4.5 Consideration of Traffic Circulation

A proposed development adds to and alters traffic circulation and mode. In approving it, care has to be taken that such changes — actually or likely to arise from the new development — will be appropriately accommodated by the existing infrastructure and traffic management systems in place. Regard should be had to surrounding land uses and other built environment features. For instance, it would be bad planning to allow development of a liquor outlet at a busy traffic intersection.

#### 2.4.6 Uncoordinated Development

Permit issuance is a key instrument for facilitating coordinated development. Where coordination is a conscious result being sought, a Local Planning Authority will use permit issuance to allow proper sequencing of development including provision of appropriate services. For instance, commercial and other job-creating land uses are best encouraged before any other land uses. Where permits are issued in an uncoordinated manner the resultant development will equally be uncoordinated. Once issued, all developments should be as per approved plans to avoid uncoordinated development. Further, compliance with statutory schemes or zoning regulations should be enforced.

### 2.5 Issuance of the Development Permit

Once planning permission to develop is granted by Council, Town Planning officials issue a permit, a legal document. The permit should be drafted very carefully to specify an applicant's development rights and obligations. In this respect a permit should contain the following:

- File reference number.
- Permit number.
- Name of applicant.
- Stand number or other legally distinct description of the land concerned.
- Description of the permitted development.
- Reference to the relevant development conditions with copy attached.
- An outline of the applicant's rights and obligations within planning law.
- The date of issue and the signature of the issuing officer.

It is recommended that the Development Permit Format shown in [Annexure 6](#annexure-6--development-permit-format) is followed as closely as possible. [Annexure 7](#annexure-7--notes-attached-to-permit) is the recommended format for notes attached to a Permit.

### 2.6 Planning Conditions

All applications are approved by Council with development conditions. These vary but in all instances must relate directly to the proposed development and its proper implementation. They relate to the test to which a development is put by the Local Planning Authority with regard to:

i. Necessity
ii. Relevance to planning
iii. Enforceability
iv. Precision
v. Reasonableness

Council sets development conditions that will ensure the highest standard of development for the community in its area of jurisdiction.

## 3.0 Development Enforcement

### 3.1 Introduction

The law sets out the procedure to be followed by a Local Planning Authority in deciding on development applications. The mechanisms defined in the law allow Local Planning Authorities to regulate development. This is how they are able to protect and/or advance individual rights in society based on facilitating and managing orderly development. Development enforcement is about managing the processes leading to and beyond the granting of permits. It thus involves ensuring that the Local Planning Authority has a sound understanding of relevant policy and law, the local, national and international development context, as well as the aspirations of the residents of its area of jurisdiction.

### 3.2 Illegal Development

Based on the law, illegal development is unauthorised, or its implementation overstepped any duly issued development conditions. This is development that will not have gone through the consideration, approval, monitoring and post-implementation management processes of a Local Planning Authority or other relevant state institutions with a planning purview.

The carrying out of development without planning permission is illegal and this must be controlled by the Local Planning Authority. Section 32 of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12] sets out the procedures for dealing with unauthorised development. If any person (natural or juristic) commences any activity without a duly issued development permit, or ignores the terms of a permit, conditions of title, or develops in a way which is different from approved plans or permit given, they would be committing an offence and the local authority should take action against the developer.

For instance, if any person commences construction work or changes the use of a building without first obtaining a permit, or does not build in accordance with approved plans, then an offence is being committed. The local authority may issue an enforcement order on the owner or occupier of the land. It therefore follows that any development that has taken place without permission, or is not permitted in terms of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12], is illegal and the Local Planning Authority must issue an enforcement order on the person carrying out the development.

### 3.3 Enforcement Order

An Enforcement Order is served on the owner of land and/or the person carrying out the development. If the person carrying out the development is the owner of the land then the order applies to the land and any successors in title. The Order must state:

a) The type of development that is considered illegal.
b) The policy, law or conditions that the development contravenes, citing relevant provisions.
c) Define the actions that the person must undertake to correct the illegality. The Local Planning Authority can require that:
   - i. The development be regularised, e.g. a permit application made.
   - ii. The buildings be demolished and land restored to its original state.
   - iii. The unauthorised use of a building cease.
   - iv. Any action that must be taken by the person.
d) State the date by (or period within) which the action must be completed.

An Enforcement Order comes into effect 30 days after it was formally served, unless there is an appeal made against it. The Order may be served on the person by hand, by registered post, or by fixing it to the premises if the person is not available. A Local Planning Authority may publish the Enforcement Order in a newspaper if it applies to a large group of people. It is also possible for a Local Planning Authority to use at least two notification methods to ensure that the offending person does not have an excuse regarding receipt of the Order. When the Order becomes operative, the Local Planning Authority must send a copy of the Order to the Registrar of Deeds for endorsement on the Title Deeds. An Operative Enforcement Order remains in force against the development until the remedies stated in the Enforcement Order are effected and an appropriate Development Permit has been issued to prevent the illegal development recurring. [Annexure 8](#annexure-8--format-of-enforcement-order) shows the format of an Enforcement Order.

### 3.4 Prohibition Order

Some illegal developments may be so serious and likely to cause injurious effects to the public and the environment that a Local Planning Authority must put an immediate stop to them. In such circumstances the Local Planning Authority cannot wait for the Enforcement Order to become operational (i.e. 30 days). In such cases the Local Planning Authority can issue a Prohibition Order. A Prohibition Order must:

i. Be served together with or immediately after the Enforcement Order, i.e. before the latter becomes operative.
ii. Specify a date on which the Order comes into force, notwithstanding any appeal against the Enforcement Order.
iii. Deal with the same issues as the Enforcement Order.
iv. Cease to be operative upon the Local Planning Authority withdrawing it, or when the Enforcement Order becomes operative.

The Local Planning Authority must carefully consider the use of a Prohibition Order because it may be liable to compensation if an appeal on the Enforcement Order is successful. The Local Planning Authority must also be prepared to take the necessary legal action to ensure the successful implementation of a Prohibition Order. The format of a Prohibition Order is as shown in [Annexure 9](#annexure-9--format-of-prohibition-order).

### 3.5 Non-Compliance with Orders

When an Enforcement Order becomes operational, it must be complied with. If it is not complied with by the person on whom it is served, the person is committing a criminal offence. The Local Planning Authority can prosecute a person who is not complying with an Enforcement Order and, if convicted by the Criminal Court, the person is liable to pay an appropriate fine, to serve an appropriate prison term, or both.

If a Local Planning Authority requires that the development must be removed, it can send its employees onto the land to implement the Enforcement Order provisions. Since the Local Planning Authority will be spending money, it may take legal action to get back the money that it has spent.

It is very important for the Local Planning Authority to give its employees instructions in writing so that the employee and the person against whom the action is taken know exactly what he/she is authorised to do. If an employee acts negligently or beyond their powers, the Local Planning Authority could be liable for compensation.

### 3.6 Appealing Local Planning Authority Decisions

Any development management/control decision by a Local Planning Authority may be appealed against. An appeal is made to the Administrative Court and must be made within 30 days of the date of the decision. The Administrative Court will only entertain appeals on town planning grounds or on unlawful and unreasonable conduct by a Local Planning Authority. An appeal can seek to have the Administrative Court suspend a Local Planning Authority's decision (permit refusal, enforcement order or notice). Where an appeal is successful, the decision of the Administrative Court is final. It must be noted that a decision of a Local Planning Authority can only be challenged by an appeal to the Administrative Court.

### 3.7 Development Management/Control Checklist

Local Planning Authority officials deal with many development applications. To streamline processes of determining the applications, the Handbook suggests a checklist. It comprises explanatory notes on what has to be done in receiving and determining applications. This can be represented in the form of a flow chart so that each step follows the next, from a permit application to an appeal. Further to this, each of the steps in the checklist is individually important.

**Step 1:** Council receives application through Town Planning services and checks its completeness.

**Step 2:** Town Planning does due diligence on the application, which entails checking the files, compatibility and statutory provisions.

**Step 3:** Acknowledgement of complete application and drafting adverts for publicity (abutting owners' notification and consultation, submission of proof of notification and publicity for Special Consideration Application).

**Step 4:** Internal and external consultations (it is encouraged that, in the spirit of Ease of Doing Business, this will be done through a one-stop-shop process).

**Step 5:** Advise applicants of any objections or representations with requirement for a response within 14 days from the date of receipt.

**Step 6:** Report to Council Committee if it is a Section 26(3) application, for a recommendation.

**Step 7:** Council Resolution.

**Step 8:** Issuance of Permit with conditions as informed by representations and objections and also guided by Town Planning standards, copying all abutting owners, objectors and all those consulted.

**Step 9:** In case of refusal, advise applicant giving reasons.

**Step 10:** Compliance monitoring/certification.

> **Editorial note [KC1]:** To compare with steps in Section 2.3 and evaluate whether the handbook needs more than one process.

Each step requires a number of functions to be performed by the Local Planning Authority official, and it is suggested that response boxes be used for checking each step. The official will tick or place a cross in each box depending on the result of the response at each step. If a step has successfully been completed a tick is placed in the box. This checklist has the value of assisting officials to check each application and the checklist should be used as a guide. ## 4.0 Building Plan Appraisal

### 4.1 Introduction

One of the main prerequisites for a development application submitted to a Local Planning Authority is a building plan. Authority staff have to be familiar with the procedures required in appraising a building plan, in addition to the content of the plan and the operative statutory plans. In order to appraise a plan, the official must know the regulations covering all buildings in Zimbabwe and, more specifically, aspects of the Model Building By-Laws, the Minimum Health Requirements, and where applicable the Liquor Licensing Board Regulations, and the provisions of the Factories and Works Act [Chapter 14:08]. In addition, when a Building Plan has been approved, each stage of its construction must then be inspected during construction.

### 4.2 Building Plan Contents

The building plan must consist of plans, drawings and specifications showing the details of the proposed building. More specifically the plan should contain:

i. Site plan
ii. Floor plan
iii. Section
iv. Elevations
v. Notes and Specifications

The arrangement of the contents of the building plan must be shown clearly. The title of the building project, the name of the client and the place (property description) of the project must be shown on the building plan, together with the date and name of designer/architect. A typical Building Plan is shown in Figure 1.

> **Figure 1 — A typical Building Plan** *(diagram in original PDF)*

#### 4.2.1 Floor Plan

The floor plan must show the position, form, dimension and materials used in every part of the building.

> **Figure 2 — Typical Floor Plan** *(diagram in original PDF)*

It is important for the Council official processing the application to note the following:

a) Every wall, window, door, and cupboard must be dimensioned in millimetres (mm), and these measurements need to be checked carefully. The position of the doors and windows is very important for air circulation.
b) All rooms need to be named in terms of their purpose, e.g. a bedroom, storeroom, reception etc. If the building has more than one floor, each floor plan must be shown.
c) The overall dimensions of the building need to be placed at the edge of the floor plan.
d) Air bricks for ventilation need to be indicated on the elevations. A minimum of two air vents per room is required and they should not be spaced more than 2 m apart. In case of terraced buildings (shops), air vents must be installed on internal walls to allow for cross ventilation.
e) Proposed water and sewage connections to the building must be shown.
f) Changes to the floor levels, steps and staircases must be shown.
g) Window sizes must be measured and compared to the individual floor areas of each room. The minimum size of window permitted, excluding frames, must be 10% of the total floor area of the room. Window sizes and doors are usually contained in a manual of fittings issued by a manufacturer and are assigned codes, e.g. D2H or C4H.
h) Any concrete works or other structural work requiring reinforcement must have detailed drawings and must be signed by a registered Structural Engineer.
i) Existing buildings and proposed buildings, if an extension, need to be clearly shown on the floor plan. The usual colour coding for building plans is:

| Element | Colour |
|---|---|
| Proposed work for bricks and masonry | Red |
| Proposed concrete work | Green |
| Proposed iron or steel work | Blue |
| Proposed woodwork | Yellow |
| Proposed sewers | Brown |

j. Where sections are taken off the floor plan, they must be clearly shown as A–A, or B–B, etc.
k. The floor plan is usually drawn to a scale of 1:50 or 1:100 depending on the size of the building. The scale must be shown on the drawing.
l. The position and number of electrical fittings such as the main switchboard, light switches and light fittings, lifts, generators, etc. must be shown on the floor plan.

#### 4.2.2 Sections

The purpose of a section is to show how the building is to be constructed, what materials are to be used, the foundation, the height of the building and other relevant structural details. The section should be clearly drawn and is usually done at a scale of 1:50 for clarity. The Council official should begin at the bottom of the drawing (foundations) and work upwards through the walls to the roof details in order to be systematic. A typical section is shown in Figure 3.

i. The height of the building from floor to ceiling and ridge must have a minimum of 2 400 mm (2,4 m) between floor and ceiling.
ii. Foundations are shown on the sections and should be 3 times the wall thickness. A 230 mm wall will require a 690 mm foundation and these should be properly dimensioned.
iii. The depth of foundation (sloping ground level) should not be less than 700 mm below ground level and the material to be used must be specified.
iv. Damp Proof Course (DPC) is usually positioned at the floor slab level between the bricks or blocks, and sometimes at the level of the window sill.
v. The type of foundation will depend on soil types and the section must indicate any abnormal soils such as heaving clays. These will require engineer-designed deeper and more expensive foundations.
vi. The type of floor construction can vary with the type of buildings and usually consists of a concrete slab of 100 mm thickness over a compacted hard-core (or soil with rocks) approximately 75 mm or 150 mm thick. On sloping sites the ground level will have to be shown because there will be excavation of soil to make sure the floor is level. The floor slab can be covered with a 20 mm or 30 mm cement screed (grano topping) on which floor tiles or carpets may be placed. These details would be shown on the section.
vii. The wall thickness and foundation depth must be shown on the section, and also brick size and type. Outer walls of buildings are usually 230 mm wide and internal walls are 115 mm if the bricks are industrial and farm bricks, provided they are plastered both sides. Concrete blocks at a size of 150 mm and 100 mm are being used more frequently by builders and should be shown on the section.
viii. The floor plan should also include access for people with disabilities in the form of a ramp for public and commercial buildings.

#### 4.2.4 Roof Details

Roof details should be shown in detail and may vary considerably with the type of building. A roof has to have a pitch (or angle) to allow for the runoff of rain. The pitch is usually 11½% upwards (depending on roof materials used) and is made by roofing companies which specialise in roof trusses. The roof truss is a system of timber or metal rafters bolted together to allow for the roof sheets or purlins and tiles to be laid. It is desirable to have an overhang of the roof of at least 0,6 m over the outside wall of a building to provide shade and protection from the wind and rain. The roof truss is fixed to the wall by a wall plate and the timber is tied onto the wall with galvanized hoop irons, to prevent the roof being blown off in high winds. The roof sheets can be made from corrugated iron or asbestos cement, made to specification by the manufacturers, and are in varying lengths.

The minimum length of the asbestos cement sheet is 1,8 m but usually the 2,1 m or 2,4 m sheets are used. The AC sheets are attached to the roof trusses on purlins (75 mm × 50 mm), approximately 1 500 mm apart. In areas of high wind velocity it might be necessary to have wind braces or lateral timber beams to support the truss and roof sheets.

If the roof is laid with tiles or slates, the purlins are closer together (approximately 300 mm apart) so that the tiles can be laid. Guttering is used for the disposal of the rain water.

#### 4.2.5 Plumbing

All sanitary fittings such as shower, bath, geyser etc. must be shown on the section. If the building is supplied with piped water and sewage disposal, the details of the water closet (WC) and sewer pipes connecting into the main sewers or septic tank must be shown. If a septic tank is used, a section must be provided of the septic tank and soakaway to ensure the correct capacity.

> **NOTE:** The toilets normally used in Zimbabwe have a water cistern with about 11 litres/flush. Many countries today install toilets with a volume per flush of about 6–9 litres/flush and sometimes, under special conditions, toilets with a flush of only 1–2 litres are used. When looking at the total average consumption for a household, it is possible to reduce the consumption by 15% in a high density area if the volume of the water/flush is about 7–8 litres instead of 11 litres. (Try to reduce the volume/flush in existing toilets, e.g. the floating valve level.)

#### 4.2.6 Elevations

There are four elevations needed for every plan (and the description must include cardinal directions, i.e. East, West, North and South):

- Front
- Rear
- Side elevations

The elevations must strictly reflect the floor plan and the section so that the building can be shown as it will be constructed on the ground. A typical elevation is shown in Figure 9.

> **Figure 9 — Typical Elevation** *(diagram in original PDF)*

The external finish of the walls and positions of the windows (opening and non-opening) and doors, all roofing material, guttering, down pipes and air vents etc. should be shown on the drawings. It is important for the Council official to look at the external appearance of the building and the materials used to ensure it blends in with the environment for which it is intended; for example, corrugated iron roofs and unplastered or unpainted walls (excluding some types of bricks) should perhaps be avoided if they detract from the surrounding buildings and can become visually unpleasant.

The finished ground level and Natural Ground Level (NGL) must be shown on the elevation so that the extent of excavation can be assessed. Boundary lines and walls must be shown on the elevation at the same scale as the floor plan.

#### 4.2.7 Site Plan

The site plan has to show the position of the principal building and outbuildings in relation to the boundaries of the stand and/or existing buildings. It is usually drawn at a different scale to that of the floor plan. Elevations and sections can be:

- 1:100 to 1:500 if the site is less than 1 hectare in extent, or
- 1:500 to 1:2000 if the site is more than 1 hectare. Where necessary, inserts can be used to show detail.

It can be included on the building plan or be a separate drawing depending on the size. Figure 10 shows a typical site plan.

> **Figure 10 — Typical Site Plan** *(diagram in original PDF)*

More specifically, the site plan shall include the following:

i. The exact location of the site with respect to the name or stand number, names of the surrounding roads, and the dimensions of all boundaries on the site including any cadastral or survey information (pegs).
ii. The nature and position of all existing natural features such as water sources, rivers, rocky outcrops, trees, and all existing man-made features such as electricity lines, telephone lines, sewers, water mains, and cables. The point of access into a public road can be indicated on the stand boundary.
iii. The position of any building lines that are applicable from a Town Planning Scheme, Master, Local or Development Plan. The building lines vary considerably from road frontages and side boundaries. It is very important for the Council official to check these building lines with their relevant town plan.
iv. The existing buildings on the site and on surrounding land must be shown on the site plan (usually hatched). Where steep land is to be encountered, the site plan must show existing contours or levels.
v. If the development involves a building with septic tank and soakaway, the position of the septic tank and soakaway in relation to the building must be shown. A septic tank is usually a minimum of 10 m away from any building.
vi. The plan must show the position of north, and it is important for the building to have the correct orientation. Usually buildings in Zimbabwe have a northerly or north-easterly orientation, which allows for the building to be cool in summer and warm in winter. The location of entrances will also be shown.

#### 4.2.8 Notes and Specifications

A building plan is never complete without notes and specifications to explain how the building is to be constructed. Usually the Notes and Specifications are contained on the individual drawings of Section, Elevation and Floor Plan, but can also be listed as a separate section. The Notes and Specifications should provide the following:

i. The type of materials to be used in all of the building. Each building may use different materials and these have to be specified on the building plan.
ii. The Council official may find that some of the material to be used is undesirable and may request a change to the type of material specified. Generally the materials will apply to concrete, walls, paint, plaster, timber, roof sheeting, sanitation fittings etc.
iii. The specifications give the building a certain quality of finish and therefore provide a standard of construction.
iv. The Notes and Specifications will provide a guide to the building contractor for the construction of the building. The building plan will not achieve its purpose if it cannot provide these guidelines to the builder.
v. The specifications can provide specialised details of the building which cannot be shown on a normal plan, section, elevation or a site plan. A building may require a specialised roofing plan or foundation plan, specialised reinforced concrete work, timber cladding or thatch.

If specialised work is required, the Notes and Specifications must be included on the building plan, or may be shown separately. Typical notes are shown in Figures 11, 12 and 13.

> **Figure 11 — Typical Notes on Building Plans** *(diagram in original PDF)*
> **Figure 12 — Typical Notes on Specifications** *(diagram in original PDF)*
> **Figure 13 — Elevation of Lintel** *(diagram in original PDF)*

A Council official may not understand some of the details of the Specifications and may request clarification from the developer, and will have to consult specialists (architects, quantity surveyors or engineers).

The Notes and Specifications also provide the dimensions for each part of the building. This is very important because it will indicate the size of building and of specific materials to be used such as roof trusses, purlins, bricks/blocks, foundations, etc.

All building plans are drawn with a provision for revision or amendment. The Notes and Specifications must indicate a space for the revisions and amendments to the plan.

### 4.3 Building Plans Relating to Factories

Where a plan relates to premises to be used as a factory, provisions of the Factories and Works Act [Chapter 14:08] should be complied with, in addition to other requirements that apply.

Section 9(4) states: *"Where an application is made to any local authority or officer for the approval, in terms of any enactment, of any plans for the erection, rebuilding or alteration of any building used or intended to be used for a factory, such local authority or officer, as the case may be, shall submit such plans to an inspector for examination by him and shall not approve such plans unless the inspector has notified the local authority or officer, as the case may be, in writing that he considers the building will be suitable for use as a factory of the nature proposed."*

A local authority is defined by the Factories and Works Act [Chapter 14:08] as a city council, municipal council or town council. A factory is defined as premises on which more than 5 persons perform functions that make, alter, repair, renovate, ornament, paint, spray, polish, finish etc. any article for sale. It is suggested that officials use their discretion when appraising building plans of an industrial nature and call on the Factories Inspectorate where necessary.

### 4.4 Building Inspectorate

Once a building plan has been approved by a local authority, the building is constructed by a building contractor and the local authority must perform the function of Building Inspectorate.

This function is extremely important to ensure that the building conforms to the building plan that has been approved. Therefore it is necessary for a local authority to understand the procedure, periods of inspections, certificates, criteria used, and to have the personnel needed for inspection.

### 4.5 Building Plan Appraisal Checklist

The appraisal of building plans is a difficult task, and the provision of a checklist may assist the Council officials on a day-to-day basis. The checklist that has been devised comprises the following:

i. The procedure of dealing with an application from receipt to approval and the issuing of a Certificate of Occupation.
ii. A number of items to check at each stage.
iii. The receipt of the application.
iv. The Minimum Requirements from the Health Standards, Town Planning, Liquor Licensing, Factories Inspectorate, and any other Laws that may prevail in the urban areas.
v. Contents of the Building Plan (Floor Plan, Section, Elevation and Site Plan).
vi. Approval or refusal with various options (extensions, time, conditions, etc.).
vii. Building inspection stages and issue of a Completion Certificate.
viii. Each item to be checked at each stage has a response box where the Council official may tick/cross when the stage is complete. Where necessary, the checklist has space for notes to be added when specific requirements are needed at the different stages. ## 5.0 Other Standard Requirements for Buildings

### 5.1 Introduction

There are a number of standards and regulations in Zimbabwe which apply to any new building. In order to assist in the understanding of the relevance of these standards and regulations, brief notes are given for Health Minimum Requirements, Model Building By-Laws, Housing, Liquor Licensing and the Factories and Works minimum requirements. Local Planning Authorities are encouraged to obtain full copies of the regulations, standards and guidelines from the relevant organisations for internal reference.

### 5.2 Common Minimum Health Requirements

All Local Planning Authorities are expected to have their own public health by-laws. However, in the absence of such, the Ministry of Health and Child Care Minimum Requirements for Premises shall be applicable. The standards cover all aspects of public safety and hygiene in buildings. The available minimum requirements apply to crèches, butcheries, general dealers, eating houses, schools, bakeries, bottle stores, beer halls and hairdressers' shops among others. Although they have not been gazetted, they are used widely by Environmental Health Practitioners and may be enforceable by law. There are some common standards for all the uses and some that are unique to the particular use. These are listed separately.

#### 5.2.1 Ventilation

The window area in each room should not be less than 10% of the floor area of any habitable room to allow for natural lighting. At least 50% of the windows should be made to open so that there is adequate cross ventilation in each room of the building.

#### 5.2.2 Sanitation and Refuse

All buildings shall be connected to the Council water supply or a water supply approved by the Council. Water closets (toilets) connected either to town sewers or a septic tank must be provided for use by staff and visitors. All public toilets should be accessible to people with disabilities and also be gender sensitive, e.g. provision of nappy changing stations and urinals or commodes. The number of water closets (WC) is determined by the number of persons occupying and/or using the building. The guideline is **1 WC per 16 persons**. Except for a family dwelling, there must be separate toilets for men and women.

All waste water must be discharged into the sewers or septic tanks and all refuse must be disposed of into standard refuse receptacles. An approved refuse receptacle area must be provided.

> **NB:** All the plumbing works are to be done by qualified and competent artisans registered with the Local Planning Authority.

#### 5.2.3 Hygiene

A reticulated water supply shall be provided. A wash hand basin with an adequate supply of piped water connected thereto shall be provided for use by staff. Suitable and adequate dust-proof storage shall be provided. All counters and tables shall be constructed of stainless steel, concrete or other suitable impervious material.

#### 5.2.4 Floor Area

The minimum floor area required in each building type:

| Building type | Minimum floor area |
|---|---|
| Crèches | 3 m² playing floor area per child, 8,3 m² resting area per child, and 17 m² per infant *(for further discussion by the house; refer to relevant statutory instrument)* |
| Bakery and Butchery | 30 m² |
| Bottle store | 20 m² |
| Eating house | 30 m² eating area and 14 m² kitchen area |
| Schools | 60 m² per classroom and 3,75 m² per child in a hostel |
| Hotels | 9 m² for single bed rooms or 13 m² for double bed rooms |
| General Dealer | 27 m² |
| Beerhalls | 40 m² |

#### 5.2.5 Construction Detail

**5.2.5.i Wall**

All walls shall be constructed on solid foundations, which shall not be less than three times the thickness of the superimposed wall, carried up to a plinth which shall be not less than 15 cm above the ground level and which shall be constructed of burnt brick or pre-cast concrete blocks, and shall be not less than 3,30 m in height as measured from the floor to the wall plate. All external walls should be a minimum of 230 mm if burnt brick and 150 mm if concrete blocks, and the internal surface of walls may be plastered with cement mortar.

**5.2.5.ii Floor**

The floor shall be a minimum of 100 mm concrete cast on 150 mm of rammed hard-core.

**5.2.5.iii Roof**

The roof shall be weatherproofed and may be constructed of corrugated iron IBR sheets, asbestos or other approved material. Adequate disposal of rainwater must be made through guttering, down piping and the slope of the roof.

**5.2.5.iv Storage**

Storage of foodstuffs in food premises should be kept in a separate rodent-proofed storeroom, and any display of food shall be contained in a properly constructed, dust-proof and fly-proof display cabinet.

### 5.3 Minimum Health Requirements for Specific Buildings

#### 5.3.1 Bakery

The openings of all oven furnaces shall be situated outside the bake houses and shall be not less than 2 m from any door or window in the bake house or storeroom.

The premises shall not form part of, or communicate directly with, any other premises in which processes other than those connected with the handling of food are carried out.

Approved facilities shall be provided for the washing and sterilising of recipes and other containers used in the handling of food.

#### 5.3.2 Restaurants, Hotel, Tea Room & Boarding House

Kitchen accommodation, which includes wash-up areas, shall be provided with a floor area of not less than one third of the floor area of the dining room.

Approved facilities for cleaning, cooking and handling of food shall be provided.

Adequate ventilation for the effective removal of hot air and fumes shall be provided.

Approved sanitary accommodation shall be provided for use by the patrons where food is consumed on the premises.

A dust-proof and well-ventilated ceiling shall be provided. The external doors shall have rodent-proof material covering the door and a concrete apron must be provided for at least 150 mm from the door.

A stainless steel sink shall be provided for the washing of implements and there shall be a supply of hot water to keep all linen, crockery and utensils in a hygienic condition. Refuse shall be stored in approved receptacles. The kitchen area shall have means to extract hot air and fumes.

#### 5.3.3 Butcheries

The internal surface of all walls shall be tiled with white glazed tiles to a height of 1,8 metres from the floor. A stainless steel sink shall be provided and there shall be an available supply of hot water for washing counters, implements, floors, walls and the hands of the persons working in the butchery.

All external doors and windows in the butchery shall be fly-proofed by means of gauze netting. The doors shall open outwards and be self-closing. Rodent-proof material shall cover the bottom of the door. The thresholds of the door shall be constructed of concrete approximately 150 mm wide.

Sufficient metal hanging rails and rust-proof meat hooks to accommodate all meat used in the building shall be provided at not less than 0,75 m from any wall of the shop.

A thermostatically controlled refrigerated room shall be provided with a capacity of not less than six cubic metres, with fitted hanging rails or shelves or racks of non-corrodible material.

#### 5.3.4 General Dealers Shops

There are no unique standards applicable, and those standards that are common to all uses apply to General Dealers/Shops.

#### 5.3.5 Sanitary Requirements for Colleges and Schools

Water-borne toilets should be provided in the following ratios:

| Fitting | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|
| Water closets | 1 per 25 pupils | 1 per 20 pupils |
| Urinals | 1 per 25 pupils | — |
| Wash hand basins | 1 per 50 pupils | 1 per 30 pupils |

An overhead water tank should be installed as a contingency measure when there is no water. A kitchen with impervious floor, dust-proof ceiling, adequate cooking and refrigeration facilities, sufficient washing and refuse disposal facilities should be provided.

A dining room with impervious floors, adequate tables and chairs and sufficient utensils should be provided. Separate dormitories for each sex with a floor space of not less than 3,75 m² for each pupil should be provided. The dormitories should contain separate single beds, adequate storage facilities for each child, suitable separate ablution and sanitary facilities, and adequate hot and cold water.

### 5.4 Model Building By-Laws (1977)

The Model Building By-Laws were prepared for use by Local Authorities in Zimbabwe. The Minister of Local Government, Rural and Urban Development published the By-Laws in RGN 41 of 1978 and an amendment in S.I. 310 of 1985 in terms of Section 183 of the Urban Councils Act [Chapter 19:08]. Through Statutory Instrument 37 of 2016, the Model Building By-Laws of 1977 were amended to reduce the time it should take a local authority to approve or condemn a building plan from 35 days to 14 days.

The By-Laws are guidelines for use by the local authorities in the construction, alteration and additions to buildings, as well as for installation of water supply and sewerage systems. Many of the chapters provide technical information on the standards to which building and sewerage works shall be undertaken.

Once formally adopted by a local authority, the Model Building By-Laws become operative in the area of jurisdiction of the Local Authority. The powers contained within the By-Laws are enforceable and can be used in a Court of Law.

### 5.5 Minimum Housing Standards

The Ministry of Local Government, Public Works, and National Housing formulated a set of minimum standards for housing in order that every family is housed in adequate and decent housing. The minimum standards are:

i. The minimum stand size is 150 m², usually 10 m by 50 m. (Also consider densification as provided by Circular 70 of 2004.)
ii. The minimum plinth size of a house is 50 m² and each room shall be larger than 6 m².
iii. The house shall have a minimum of 2 rooms consisting of 1 bedroom, 1 kitchen, and a flush toilet and shower.

Each house can vary in design depending on individual tastes. The Ministry of Local Government, Public Works and National Housing has designed a number of standard houses for use by local authorities, parastatals and individuals. General criteria are:

i. All houses are to be free standing, detached or semi-detached.
ii. All houses to have a separate room for preparing food.
iii. All houses to have a toilet and a shower which can share the same space but have separate drains and have sufficient room to incorporate a bath tub in the future.
iv. All approved house plans to show a minimum of kitchen, bathroom and 1 other room.
v. All houses to be expandable to have scope for transformation.
vi. All houses to be of single storey design to facilitate construction by aided self-help techniques.
vii. All plans to permit phased construction without excessive demolition work.
viii. All houses to be built of burnt brick, stabilised soil, or cement block masonry units with metal, tile or asbestos cement roofing.
ix. Where water reticulation and sewerage disposal is provided, the bathroom and toilets can be provided in the house.

The house shall be placed on the stand in such a way that there is space at the front, back and sides of the house. The minimum space requirements, called building lines, are usually 3 m at the front, 2 m at the back and 1 m at the sides. If a wall has no doors or windows at the side, it may be constructed at 0,5 m from the stand boundary.

### 5.6 Liquor Licensing Regulations

The Liquor Licensing Board was set up under the Liquor Act [Chapter 14:12] for the purpose of controlling the issuance of liquor licences. In order to provide guidelines for the Board, Regulations have been gazetted for a variety of liquor outlets. There are minimum standards which are common to liquor outlets. These include:

i. Water supplies
ii. Ventilation
iii. Lighting
iv. Sanitation
v. Refuse
vi. Fire
vii. Insurance
viii. Communication
ix. Uniforms
x. Maintenance
xi. Staff accommodation (where applicable)

More specific standards are required for hotels, restaurants, nightclubs, airports, theatres, sports and cultural clubs and bottle stores. When a liquor licence is required by an applicant, plans are submitted to the Liquor Licensing Board for approval. The premises to be licensed must conform to the appropriate regulations. Local Authority officials should obtain copies of the appropriate Liquor Licensing Regulations and minimum requirements.

The regulations also make provision for specifying the population capacity and sanitary conveniences required in types of public buildings such as cinemas, theatres, assembly halls, bars, restaurants, nightclubs and stadia. These vary with the size of building and give guidelines for the number of persons per square metre of floor space and the number of water closets, urinals and wash hand basins per persons. These are usually read in conjunction with the Model Building By-Laws (1977).

It is recommended that liquor outlets at any given commercial centre shall not exceed 25% of the number of licensed premises of any type.

### 5.7 Approval of Building Plans

An appropriate council official (usually the Director of Works or equivalent) must sign the building plans once they have been approved. It is advisable to have a simple stamp made which shows the approval of building plans.

Usually a local authority gives an applicant 12 months in which to begin construction. It is important to make sure that the construction has commenced in that period.

When the plans are approved, the local authority may endorse the plans for inspection purposes in addition to the approval stamp. The "inspection stamp" may be in the format presented in [Annexure 12](#annexure-12--specimen-stage-inspection-stamp).

### 5.8 Frequency of Inspection

The inspection stamp/checklist has nine stages that require endorsement of a building inspector. At each stage the building inspector must be satisfied that the contractor has done the construction according to requirements.

In many cases each inspection stage can require a number of visits to be made by the inspector. The building contractor may be required to change or improve on certain aspects of workmanship and these changes need to be verified. There is therefore a need for follow-up inspections in addition to the routine inspections.

Many building contractors and applicants will do their best to have everything on their building site to perfection only on routine and follow-up inspection days. This may result in buildings being incorrectly checked. Therefore, there is a need to do spot checks or informal inspections of the building.

The size of the building will determine the number of inspection visits needed and the frequency of visits. However, a routine check should be made twice a month on most medium-sized buildings.

The criterion used for assessing the building progress is contained in the Model Building By-Laws, and a Building Inspector needs to be familiar with its provisions. A checklist ([Annexure 14](#annexure-14--building-inspection-checklist)) is provided as a guide.

> **NB:** The Local Authority may approve the occupation of a building before the whole building has been completed, subject to meeting basic minimum sanitation requirements and any other conditions as the Local Authority may impose.

### 5.9 Occupation Certificate

The building inspection stamp will provide sufficient certification of progress in building construction. However, there is a need for the local authority to issue a Certificate of Occupation when the building has been completed and connected to the council services.

An applicant may not occupy the building until the certificate is issued by the local authority. In some local authorities there is a team comprising Building Inspectors, an Environmental Health Officer and Plumbers who do the final inspection of any building for the issuance of an occupation certificate. A Sample Certificate of Occupation is shown in [Annexure 13](#annexure-13--sample-occupation-certificate).

## 6.0 Organization & Management of the Town Planning Office

### 6.1 Introduction

The Town Planning Office (TPO) is charged with the responsibility of ensuring the orderly and progressive development of land in local authority areas. The activities pursuant to this mandate are undertaken in accordance with the provisions of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act [Chapter 29:12]. The primary functions of the TPO include:

i. Preparation and monitoring of physical development plans.
ii. Controlling the development of land having regard to proper planning standards and environmental management practices.
iii. Monitoring of development activities and enforcement of development management/control.

### 6.2 Components of Town Planning Office

A typical town planning office comprises:

**i. Development Management/Control Section** — Processes applications for planning permission; undertakes assessments and evaluation of applications in relation to the appropriate policy and development standards, and makes recommendations or decisions on these applications.

**ii. Forward Planning Section** — Responsibility for the preparation of physical development plans, the co-ordination of policy studies and direct participation in special planning projects and studies, and the development and management of information systems. The policy division provides planning and property advice to Council; develops standards and policy guidelines and prepares development briefs. Other functions include participation on committees and the collection and compilation of information on the activities of the TPO and the preparation of performance reports.

**iii. Building Inspectorate** — Key responsibility is to monitor development and to enforce planning control throughout the area of jurisdiction. The prime functions of this division can be considered to be investigation of complaints, inspection of buildings under construction and issuance of certificates of compliance.

**iv. Administration and General Services** — Provision of personnel management, administrative, clerical, stenographic and accounting functions. In bigger local authorities other units include:
1. Traffic and transportation planning
2. Estate planning
3. Land Survey
4. GIS/ICT

### 6.3 Town Planning Human Capital

The work load of the TPO has increased significantly over the years in tandem with the volume and nature of the duties that the office now performs. For the effective discharge of its mandate, the TPO needs to be staffed with the following personnel. Depending on the size, the town planning section may have the following posts:

i. The Chief Town Planner
ii. Town Planning Officers
iii. Planning Technicians
iv. Building Inspector(s)
v. Survey Technician(s)
vi. Planning/Technical Assistant(s)
vii. Planning Clerk(s)
viii. Draughtsman

The number of bodies is also dependent upon the size of the local authority.

## 7.0 Summary and Conclusions

Any job aid has a specific focus and target group. This Technical Guideline for Development Management/Control is intended to assist the development management/control practitioners in the urban local authorities to manage development. In addition to providing an illustrated review of the development management/control process, the technical guideline has provided checklists which can be used by the Local Planning Authority officials for reference. It is important to note that development management/control is not only a mechanism for orderly development in space and can be used effectively in their areas of jurisdiction. The Guideline can assist Council officials identify when planning permission is necessary and how to process the application when it is made.

> The powers given to Local Authorities in terms of the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act are wide, and the Council officials must realise these powers so that he/she may guide both developers and the general public.

As part of the development process, building plans are necessary to illustrate the development taking place. Buildings can vary considerably, from multi-storey and multi-purpose concrete structures in large urban areas to a local commercial centre. However, the rules and standards set out for building plans apply universally and the Council official must be familiar with these standards. The Council official will be able to approve or refuse building plan applications more confidently with the help of the Building Plan Appraisal Checklist. It is therefore anticipated that this Development Management/Control Guideline will go a long way in assisting Local Authorities in Zimbabwe to identify, promote, process, approve and monitor development in a more efficient and effective manner.
